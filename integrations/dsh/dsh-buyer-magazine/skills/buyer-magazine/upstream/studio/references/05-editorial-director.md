# 05 · Editorial director

## Contract

**Input:** admissible product records, buyer category/market, prior issue if supplied. **Output:** an editorial plan and a concise issue draft. **Consumer:** storyboard and copy. Optimize for a useful decision within a coffee break, not maximum SKU count.

## Set one editorial question

Choose a question the collected evidence can answer: “Which compact home accessories deserve sample review?” is useful; “The future of global sourcing” is too broad for three product pages. Write the question privately, then select one lead angle. Do not pretend to have found a trend merely because three suppliers sell similar products.

Identify whether the issue supports discovery, comparison, or active procurement. Discovery can contain more unknown terms. Active procurement needs much stronger commercial evidence. A discovery issue must not look like an approved quotation.

## Choose products by editorial utility

Use this rubric as an internal selection aid, not a public score:

| Dimension | Ask | Prefer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Relevance | Does it match the category, market, and use case? | A clear buyer application |
| Distinctiveness | Does it add a different option or constraint? | Meaningful differences over color duplicates |
| Evidence | Can the important claims be checked? | Specific source-backed details |
| Actionability | Is there a valid next step? | A product/contact/sample pathway |
| Information cost | Does it require too much explanation? | A concise story with visible limitations |

Do not rank only by evidence completeness: a promising product with an explicit missing MOQ can still be worth showing. Do not rank paid placement as an editorial recommendation without a disclosure. Keep the sponsored label adjacent to the affected product.

## Budget five minutes deliberately

Use these package editorial targets, not claims about a universal reading speed:

| Section | Target contribution |
| --- | --- |
| Lead headline and dek | 35–65 words |
| Editor note and quick signals | 70–120 words |
| Three product stories | 110–170 words each, including their main facts |
| Comparison and uncertainty notes | 80–130 words |
| Closing next step and disclosures | 30–60 words |

Aim for approximately 600–950 visible English words in the core issue. Count repeated source details and drawer text separately. Use the audit script as a warning system. A 300-word issue may be appropriate when the facts are sparse; never inflate it to a numerical target. A 1,500-word issue needs either editing or a truthful longer-read label.

Readers should learn the issue's category and utility in the first screen, see product evidence without opening every drawer, and reach a practical next step after each story. Do not make them complete a tutorial or choose filters to reveal the issue.

## Preserve editorial tension

For each selected product, write:

1. What it is.
2. Why this buyer might care.
3. The two or three facts that establish that relevance.
4. The most important unresolved question.
5. The next action.

This creates a productive rhythm: interest, evidence, constraint, action. Avoid a stream of praise. Use a short “Before you request a sample” note to turn a missing fact into a precise sourcing question.

## Gate

Read the draft without images. If the issue contains no decision value beyond the source website's own sales pitch, revise the angle. Read it with only headlines, rows, and captions. If the buyer cannot understand the options, revise the hierarchy.

**Example:** three fictional home products can illustrate a design. A real issue from a single lamp website should focus on that catalog's supported distinctions, not inherit the fixture's kitchenware and luggage stories to fill the template.
